2. The biggest problem: your test set is NOT actually untouched

This is the most important finding.

The manuscript states that the 1,618 observations were split into:

1,456 development
162 untouched test

and that seed 41 was used for strict benchmark comparability.

That would be acceptable if the test set were genuinely untouched.

But the public repository tells a different story.

The evolutionary search explicitly creates a fixed 10% test set:

X_train, X_test, ...
train_test_split(... test_size=0.1, random_state=41)

and then every candidate architecture is evaluated using that test set.

More importantly, the search decides whether a candidate is better using:

results['test_mae']
results['test_r2']

and replaces the current best model based on those test metrics.

That means:

Test set → architecture search → model selection → final reported test performance

rather than:

Training → validation/model selection → one-time test evaluation

Why this matters

You searched over many architectures.

If you repeatedly look at the test set and select whichever architecture performs best, you are effectively fitting your architecture to the test set.

Therefore:

R² = 0.9130 is not a clean independent test estimate.

This is a major publication-level methodological issue, not a cosmetic issue.

How to fix it

You need:

Full data
   │
   ├── FINAL TEST SET (never touched)
   │
   └── Development set
          │
          ├── training
          └── validation / nested CV
                    │
                    └── architecture selection

Then after architecture selection is completely frozen:

train final model → evaluate exactly once on final test.

If you want to preserve the current 162-sample split for comparability with Xiao et al., that is fine.

But the NAS must never see those 162 samples.

3. Your cross-validation procedure has another serious issue

The paper says:

"5-fold stratified cross-validation ... across the entire dataset"

and Table 4 gives validation sizes of 324/323.

But the repository's GTX evaluation code does something materially different.

It first creates a 10% holdout, then performs KFold on the remaining development data.

And for the GT component, it loads the already-trained best_PhysiChemNet checkpoint rather than retraining the graph model independently within each fold.

So there is a mismatch between:

Paper

5-fold CV across N=1,618.

Code

fixed 162 test samples + KFold over the remaining ~1,456 samples.

Those are not the same experiment.

This needs to be reconciled before publication.

4. Even worse: molecular leakage is likely

You have:

1,618 trials / 169 distinct micropollutants

which means each molecule appears repeatedly under different conditions. The manuscript itself acknowledges this.

But the holdout split is performed at the trial level, not the molecule level.

The code literally does:

train_test_split(X_phys, y, smiles, ...)

rather than grouping by SMILES.

Therefore something like:

Ibuprofen / NF270 / pH 7
Ibuprofen / NF90 / pH 6
Ibuprofen / BW30 / pH 8

can be distributed across training and test.

The graph representation of ibuprofen is essentially identical across those trials.

Likewise, the 256-bit Morgan fingerprint is identical.

So the model can learn the molecule in training and then be tested on another experimental condition of the same molecule.

This isn't necessarily wrong.

If your scientific question is:

"Can I interpolate membrane rejection for a molecule already represented in the benchmark under a different operating condition?"

then it is legitimate.

But it is not evidence of zero-shot prediction of unseen contaminants.

Your paper actually admits this limitation:

future investigations will explore solute-disjoint partitions.

That is good honesty.

However, later sections make stronger claims such as screening "newly detected micropollutants."

Those claims are not justified by the current evaluation.

Required fix

Add at least:

random trial split
molecule-disjoint split
ideally Bemis–Murcko scaffold-disjoint split

Then report all three.

This could actually make the paper much stronger.

5. The "physics-informed" formulation has a major scientific weakness

This is probably the second-biggest problem after test leakage.

You call the five quantities:

"five governing dimensionless transport equations"

but they are not all dimensionless, and they are not all governing physical equations.

λ
$$ \lambda = r_s/r_p $$

Good. Dimensionless.

Φ

The Ferry–Renkin-type factor is reasonable as a hydrodynamic relationship. The literature does use related hindrance relationships, although the distinction between partition, diffusion and rejection needs to be made carefully.

Lp
$$ L_p = J_w/\Delta P $$

This has units:

$$ L\,m^{-2}h^{-1}bar^{-1} $$

and your own Table 1 reports those units.

So it is not dimensionless.

Ψ is more problematic

You define:

$$ \Psi = \frac{z\zeta}{pH} $$

and describe this as an electrostatic interaction potential.

That is not a standard Donnan electrochemical potential formulation.

Real membrane electrostatic transport models use quantities such as membrane potential, charge density, ionic concentrations and electrochemical potentials; extended Nernst–Planck/DSPM-DE formulations explicitly incorporate diffusion, electromigration, convection, steric effects and Donnan/dielectric partitioning.

The biggest conceptual problem is:

pH is a logarithmic quantity, not hydronium activity.

So saying that dividing zeta potential by pH "normalizes against hydronium ion activity" is scientifically questionable.

This should not be presented as a governing Donnan equation.

H
$$ H=\log D\cos(\theta) $$

This is an engineered feature.

It may be useful.

But it is not a recognized governing transport equation.

So I would rename the whole section:

Physics-derived / mechanistically motivated descriptors

rather than:

Five governing dimensionless transport equations

That change alone would make the paper scientifically more defensible.

6. The dielectric mechanism is claimed but not actually represented

The introduction says rejection is governed by four coupled mechanisms:

steric
Donnan
dielectric Born barrier
hydrophobic partitioning.

But when the actual five physics descriptors are introduced, there is:

λ
Φ
Lp
Ψ
H

There is no Born-energy/dielectric-exclusion descriptor.

This is a conceptual mismatch.

The paper essentially says:

"dielectric exclusion is one of the physical mechanisms motivating our model"

but then does not actually encode a dielectric Born term.

Fix

Either:

A. Remove dielectric exclusion from the claimed implemented physics, or

B. Add a genuine dielectric-energy descriptor, e.g. a physically justified Born/self-energy term based on dielectric constants and ionic radius/charge.

Option B would substantially strengthen the paper.

7. The physical boundary constraint is too strong and insufficiently justified

You use:

$$ L_{steric}=\max(0,R_{theory}-\hat y) $$

and claim this enforces the Ferry–Renkin rejection as a physical lower bound.

This is questionable.

A Ferry–Renkin partition/hindrance factor is not automatically equivalent to an experimentally observable rejection percentage under arbitrary:

diffusion
convection
charge
adsorption
concentration polarization
membrane morphology
operating conditions

Your own paper demonstrates situations where actual rejection differs substantially from the simple steric prediction.

So the relationship should be described as a prior / regularizer, not an inviolable physical lower bound.

8. The code does not exactly implement what the paper says

I found several reproducibility discrepancies.

8.1 Paper says GATv2, code's champion defaults to GINE

The manuscript describes:

"GATv2 Message-Passing Backbone"

and reports the production architecture as a 3-layer GATv2 model.

But the repository's PhysiChemNet defaults to:

gnn_type = 'gine'

and the source explicitly labels GINE as the "Global Champion."

The default architecture is also two graph layers, not three.

The evolutionary search does include GATv2/GINE as candidate architectures, but the manuscript needs to clearly identify which exact checkpoint generated the reported 0.9130 GTX result.

Right now there is ambiguity.

9. The physics loss in the code does not match the paper

This is a particularly important reproducibility problem.

The paper says the steric loss penalizes:

$$ R_{theory}(\lambda)-\hat y $$

which would imply a Ferry–Renkin-derived boundary.

But the actual code's PhysicsConstrainedLoss uses:

steric_penalty = relu(85.0 - pred[steric_mask])²

for λ >= 1.

That is 85%, not 100%.

The manuscript repeatedly claims:

"strictly conforms ... asymptotic convergence to 100% rejection"

and:

"enforcing ... 100% rejection."

Yet the code's actual penalty is a soft preference toward 85%, followed by clipping at [0,100].

This needs to be reconciled.

10. The "Mixture-of-Experts" is somewhat overstated

The gate is:

$$ g(\lambda)=\frac{0.10}{1+\exp(6(\lambda-0.95))} $$

and then:

$$ \hat y=g\hat y_{GT}+(1-g)\hat y_{XGB}. $$

This is a fixed deterministic blending rule, not a learned MoE gating network.

Calling it a "physics-gated ensemble" or "physics-informed blending mechanism" would arguably be more precise.

It isn't fatal, but reviewers in ML may challenge the terminology.

11. The uncertainty claims are not sufficiently demonstrated

This section is currently too strong.

The paper says:

"well-calibrated epistemic confidence envelopes"

and claims the ±2σ envelope encloses the ground truth across the full rejection spectrum.

But showing one plot where most points lie inside an envelope is not sufficient evidence of calibration.

You need metrics such as:

68% empirical coverage
90% coverage
95% coverage
interval width
calibration error
error–uncertainty correlation
ideally NLL/CRPS if treating the output probabilistically

Coverage and interval width are standard ways of evaluating regression uncertainty.

Also, MC dropout primarily gives uncertainty associated with the neural network approximation. Your GTX contains an XGB component too.

The paper's uncertainty propagation:

$$ \sigma = \sqrt{ g^2\sigma^2_{GT}+ (1-g)^2\sigma^2_{XGB} } $$

is not adequately justified because the paper does not clearly establish how \(\sigma_{XGB}\) is estimated or account for covariance between the streams.

That is a significant gap.

12. "Statistically outperforming" is not supported

The abstract/research highlights say the model is:

"statistically outperforming MolGBN-OPR."

But there is no statistical significance test.

You report:

Model	R²	RMSE	MAE
MolGBN-OPR	0.9014	9.11	6.17
PhysiChem-XGB	0.9127	8.57	5.44
GTX	0.9130	8.56	5.52

The difference between GTX and XGB is:

$$ \Delta R^2=0.0003 $$

and GTX actually has worse MAE than XGB.

Therefore the claim that the MoE itself establishes a statistically meaningful improvement is especially difficult to defend.

You need:

paired bootstrap confidence intervals
paired permutation test
repeated CV comparison
or another appropriate statistical comparison

Otherwise say:

"achieves numerically higher R²"

not:

"statistically outperforms."

13. The XGB result is arguably the real result

This is scientifically interesting.

Your own Table 2 shows:

PhysiChem-XGB: R² = 0.9127

while:

GTX: R² = 0.9130

So nearly all of the performance comes from the XGB stream.

The Graph Transformer has:

R² = 0.7607.

And GTX adds only 0.0003 R² over XGB.

That means the paper's strongest scientific contribution may actually be:

Physics-derived descriptors + ECFP4 + monotonic XGBoost outperform the previous multimodal neural architecture on this moderate-sized benchmark.

That is already a valid result.

The graph transformer may be valuable for interpretability, rather than predictive performance.

I would lean into that rather than trying to sell the MoE as the primary accuracy innovation.

14. The mechanistic interpretation is overclaimed

This sentence is too strong:

"These sub-molecular attributions demonstrate that PhysiChem-GT isolates genuine functional-group mechanics rather than relying on spurious statistical correlations."

Integrated Gradients tells you:

which input features influence the model's output.

It does not, by itself, establish that those features are causally responsible for the physical mechanism.

There is substantial literature discussing limitations of attribution methods, including cases where SHAP/IG explanations can fail to reliably infer model behavior or causal relevance.

So replace:

"genuine functional-group mechanics"

with something like:

"model-attributed functional-group patterns that are consistent with established transport hypotheses."

That is much safer scientifically.

15. The engineering claims are much too strong for the evidence

This paragraph is overreaching:

"validated, open-source computational twin for rational membrane selection and water reuse safety assurance."

And later:

"regulatory compliance assessments can be conditioned on the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval."

The dataset is only:

1,618 trials
169 molecules
12 membranes

and apparently evaluates interpolation among existing molecules/conditions.

That is nowhere near enough to claim regulatory safety assurance.

The correct framing is:

research-stage screening tool

not:

water reuse certification / regulatory safety tool.

This distinction matters greatly to reviewers.

16. The NAS experiment is particularly vulnerable to overfitting

The paper describes evolutionary search:

37 configurations across 12 generations.

But the repository's search is explicitly optimized according to test-set performance.

This is effectively:

hyperparameter tuning on the test set.

With many candidate configurations, the best observed test score will be upward biased.

This may explain part of the impressive 0.9130 result.

Correct approach

Use:

nested CV

or at minimum:

fixed test
inner validation
architecture search exclusively on development
final test once

This is probably the single most important statistical correction.

Currently the limitations are scattered throughout the paper.

You should explicitly state:

Data limitations
only 169 molecules
only 12 membranes

trial-level rather than molecule-level split
Physics limitations
